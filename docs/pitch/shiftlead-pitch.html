The problem

Every operation has things that break, and people who have to get there.

Problems surface late

Alarms sit on a dashboard nobody is watching. By the time someone looks, the order is already late.

The handoff is manual

Robot tools flag the incident and ping a chat channel. A person still decides who goes and checks the fix.

New things go live on faith

A new robot skill, route or process gets switched on after a few good runs instead of real evidence.

81 minutes on average to get production running again after a stoppage, up from 49 five years earlier.CitedSiemens, True Cost of Downtime 2024, large factories

ShiftLead takes that whole loop off the dispatcher’s plate, without sending anything to a cloud model.

“Today the robot’s own tools flag the problem and ping a chat channel. Then a dispatcher with four windows open, the alarm, the ticket queue, the calendar and a maps app, decides who goes. And recovery keeps getting slower: Siemens puts it at 81 minutes, up from 49.”

How it works

One loop, running all shift.

  1. Detect

    Code

    Nine detectors read telemetry from all 20 robots, 8 times a second.

  2. Decide

    Supervisor agent

    Log it, reschedule it, or ticket it with evidence. Urgency comes from the orders at risk.

  3. Dispatch

    Scheduler agent

    Picks the right person, based on where they are and how long it takes them to get there.

  4. Verify

    Code

    Anything new has to pass the trust gate before it touches real work.

  5. Report

    Supervisor agent

    Answers in Discord and posts the shift brief, inventory summary and action plan.

↻ Every ticket, answer and trial is logged, and the next pass starts from it.

“It’s one loop. Code detects, with nine detectors over live telemetry eight times a second. The Supervisor agent decides what each problem needs and opens a ticket with evidence. The Scheduler agent picks the right person, given where they are and their commute. Anything new must pass a statistical trust gate. Everything reports back in Discord.”

The trust gate

Nothing new goes live until the evidence says so.

“Same robot arm, same drawer task, two candidate policies. The current one failed 5 of 5 and the candidate went 15 for 15. The bandit stops spending trials on the loser, and the gate only opens when it’s 95% sure the success rate is at least 80%.”

If asked: 13 straight successes is the shortest run that opens it, and 9 of 10 is only 68%. In 3,000 simulated runs, a 95% policy passes 89% of the time in about 24 trials, a 75% policy just under the bar gets through 3.6% of the time, and a 40% policy never did. Don’t claim it “never” opens wrongly. Backup slide B2 has the tables.

The design rule

Code enforces. The model explains.

Decided in code

The model reads these and can’t override them

  • ThresholdsBattery under 15%, motor over 80 °C, stopped over 2 s
  • Problem identityOne ID per problem, one live ticket per problem
  • Deadline riskProjected finish against due time for every open order
  • Ticket statesIllegal transitions are rejected
  • TrustThe gate opens only at 95% confidence of at least 80% success
  • ReachThe agent’s sandbox can call its tools and nothing else

Decided by the model

Judgment and language

  • What matters nowWhich problems to act on, and which are one systemic issue
  • Where it goesLog it, hand it to the Scheduler, or escalate to a person
  • How urgentPriority and deadline, taken from the orders actually at risk
  • What to sayPlain-language answers, briefs and recaps for the team

“The design rule: code enforces, the model explains. Thresholds, detection, deadline risk, ticket states and the trust gate are all code, and the agent’s sandbox can only reach its own tools. The model does the judgment and the writing.” If asked: when the agent tries the presenter controls, it gets a 403.

When it doesn’t work out

failed→escalated→a person takes it
  • SchedulerAcknowledges, reschedules, sets who is going and their ETA

For “how do the agents talk to each other?”: tickets over HTTP on the tool service. Every change wakes the other agent through a webhook, and code rejects illegal state changes.

TakeawayTravel-aware dispatch already exists. What we add is the ready-made loop from robot alarm to a checked fix, with a trust gate for anything new, running on one box.

Don’t say “nobody does travel-aware dispatch.” Microsoft, Salesforce and ServiceNow all match on skills and location and estimate travel time.

On the fix check, say “we haven’t seen it”, not “nobody does it”. None of the products reviewed accepts a fix based on post-fix evidence; they close when the technician marks the job done.

Be exact about our own fix check. A ticket can be resolved without evidence. When the Scheduler or a person resolves one, the Supervisor is woken to re-check the robot or zone in live data. The hard, code-enforced gate is the trust gate for new skills and routes.

On-site AI isn’t unique: ServiceNow Private Stack and Siemens Industrial Copilot run on site too. Our point is the whole loop, ready-made on one box, without an IT program. If a judge says InOrbit or Formant will add dispatch: they expose webhooks, so we can sit behind them as the part that decides who goes and checks the fix.

TakeawayWe run locally because the data must stay on site. Against frontier APIs, the box also pays for itself in about 8–16 months.

Typical site, projected from today’s measurements: 1,065 model calls a day (100 handled events, briefs and chat), 32.3M prompt tokens a day with 83% cache hits (measured hit rate), 0.55M output tokens a day.

Measured on this box, 12:09–14:43 ET: 249 model calls, about 30K prompt tokens and 514 output tokens per call, about 9 calls per agent wake-up. Busiest 10 minutes: 9.8 calls a minute, up to 4 at once, nothing queued. Live agent calls had a median of 6.75 s and a p95 of 49 s; 3.4 s is a short tool-calling turn.

Cloud cost per year for that load: Claude Sonnet 5.5 $7,957 ($8,957 with cache-write premiums); gpt-6.1-sol $6,979–$7,979; Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview $8,357 before cache storage; hosted Qwen3.6-35B-A3B $485–$2,308 (OpenRouter endpoints; DeepInfra about $1,369).

Box cost per year: $6,177–$9,007 over 3 years, plus $165–$360 a year in power (140–280 W around the clock at the US commercial average). That’s an upper bound: the GPU measured 58–60 W under agent load today. Payback against frontier APIs: 8–16 months. Don’t compare with NVIDIA’s $4,699 DGX Spark directly.

Fine print: Connecticut’s penalties are maximums ($500 / $1,000 / $3,000). EU AI Act high-risk obligations were postponed to 2 Dec 2027 (law-firm summary, not checked on EUR-Lex). Running locally doesn’t remove notice duties; we present the trust gate and audit trail as compliance features.
